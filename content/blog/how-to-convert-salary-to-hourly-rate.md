## Why Converting Salary to Hourly Rate Matters

Knowing how to convert salary to hourly rate is essential any time you compare a salaried job offer to freelance work, negotiate a raise, or try to understand your true take-home per hour worked. A $80,000 salary looks different depending on whether you work 40 or 50 hours a week — and it looks very different once taxes and benefits are factored in.

This guide walks through all five steps, from the standard formula to the real take-home number.

---

## Step 1: Start With Your Annual Salary (or Hourly Rate)

The starting point depends on which direction you are converting:

- **Salary to hourly:** you need your gross annual salary before any deductions.
- **Hourly to salary:** you need your base hourly rate, not including overtime.

For this guide, two worked examples run in parallel:
- **Example A:** $80,000 annual salary → find hourly rate
- **Example B:** $25/hour → find annual salary

If you are mid-year and do not know your annualized salary, take your most recent pay stub and multiply: gross pay per paycheck × number of paychecks per year (26 for bi-weekly, 24 for semi-monthly, 52 for weekly).

---

## Step 2: Determine Your Hours Per Year

The standard assumption is **2,080 hours per year**: 52 weeks × 40 hours per week. This is the figure most employers and salary websites use, and it is the right baseline for a full-time W-2 employee.

**Example A:** $80,000 ÷ 2,080 hours = **$38.46/hour**

**Example B:** $25 × 2,080 hours = **$52,000/year**

## Step 3: Adjust for Your Actual Work Schedule

The 2,080-hour standard assumes you work every single week with no time off. In practice:

- **Federal holidays:** 11 days in 2025 → 88 hours off if your employer observes them
- **Paid vacation/PTO:** 10 days typical for new employees → 80 hours
- **Part-time or reduced schedule:** 30 hours/week = 1,560 hours/year

If you actually work 48 hours/week, your effective hourly rate is lower than the formula suggests:
- $80,000 ÷ (48 × 52) = $80,000 ÷ 2,496 = **$32.05/hour** (not $38.46)

Always use your actual hours worked, not the nominal 40/week, when comparing two job offers.

---

## Step 4: Account for Paid Time Off and Holidays

If your employer offers paid time off, your *paid* hours differ from your *worked* hours. Two ways to think about this:

- **Effective rate (paid only):** if you receive 3 weeks PTO (120 hrs) and 11 holidays (88 hrs), you work 2,080 − 120 − 88 = **1,872 hours** but get paid for 2,080. Your paid rate is still $38.46/hr, but you are effectively earning more per hour actually worked: $80,000 ÷ 1,872 = **$42.74/hr**.
- **Comparison to contractor rates:** if a contractor charges $55/hr and works the same 1,872 hours, they earn $102,960 gross — but pay their own self-employment tax (~15.3%), health insurance (~$7,000/year), and no paid time off. The salaried employee at $80,000 with benefits can easily be the better deal.

---

## Step 5: Calculate Net Hourly Rate After Taxes and Benefits

Gross hourly is only half the picture. Here is how to find your true take-home per hour.

**Example A — $80,000 salary:**
- Federal income tax (effective rate ~14%): −$11,200
- FICA (7.65%): −$6,120
- State income tax (assume 4%): −$3,200
- Total tax burden: ~25% → net annual = **$60,000 → $28.85/hour net**

**Example B — $25/hour ($52,000 gross):**
- Total tax burden at this income level: ~18–22%
- Net annual: approximately **$40,560–$42,640 → $19.50–$20.50/hour net**

These are estimates. Your actual rate depends on your state, filing status, 401(k) contributions, and other deductions. For a precise figure, run your numbers through the [CalcKit federal tax calculator](https://calckit.yaro-labs.com/tax).

## Conclusion: What to Remember

- **The standard formula** is annual ÷ 2,080 for hourly, or hourly × 2,080 for annual — a reliable starting point for any comparison.
- **Actual hours worked** often differ from 2,080; if you work 48-hour weeks regularly, your true hourly rate is lower than the formula suggests.
- **Benefits have real dollar value** — employer-sponsored health insurance, 401(k) match, and paid time off can add $10,000–$20,000+ in effective compensation annually.
- **Net hourly rate** (after taxes) is the number that matters for budgeting — gross hourly can be misleading by 20–30%.
- **Freelancers must charge more** to cover self-employment tax, benefits, and unpaid downtime — a $55/hr contractor rate is often comparable to a $65,000 salary.
